# Literature for improving the existing revision investigator

Research date: 2026-09-26. Author: Codex research worker. Status: primary-source links and scope reviewed by the coordinator; experiment implications remain proposals. This focused search informs the next investigation loop; it does not clear novelty or authorize additional runs.

The current result already demonstrates useful structure revision on the coupled challenge. Its more urgent limitation is transfer: one unnecessary control expansion and stress adoptions that lose to their own frozen incumbents on external tapes. Three noise seeds and a supplied, challenge-capable grammar cannot establish broad active-learning superiority. The following sources suggest concrete changes to this investigator rather than another detached statistical exercise.

## Seven close sources and practical implications

1. **Sloman, Oppenheimer, Broomell and Shalizi (2022), “Characterizing the robustness of Bayesian adaptive experimental designs to active learning bias.”** [Primary preprint and full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2205.13698v2). The authors examine how misspecification interacts with the sampling distribution in Bayesian adaptive design. Their linear-model and simulated nonlinear examples exhibit poorer target-distribution generalization from adaptive sampling; greater misspecification aggravates the problem in their studied settings. This is a close warning for our alternative-driven checks. **Implication:** reserve some check actions for a declared coverage distribution rather than letting all checks maximize committee separation. Compare transfer loss and useful adoptions at the same total cost. **Limit:** their setting is not our noisy, persistent, partially observed simulator, and our disagreement score is not their Bayesian information gain. Their observation-variance experiments are not permission to relabel unexplained dynamics as sensor noise. Full-text sections 1, 4, 6–8 were inspected.

2. **Olofsson, Deisenroth and Misener (ICML 2018), “Design of Experiments for Model Discrimination Hybridising Analytical and Data-Driven Approaches.”** [Primary proceedings](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v80/olofsson18a.html). The paper uses Gaussian-process surrogates to extend established experimental-design/model-discrimination procedures to mechanistic models that are difficult to handle analytically. The explicit target is discrimination among rival predictive models. **Implication:** preserve our recorded prospective predictions and selection scores, but separate “this action distinguishes candidates” from “the selected candidate predicts adequately.” A coverage check should include incumbent-versus-observation error, even when alternatives agree. **Limit:** our small symbolic programs need no GP surrogate; adopting that machinery would add cost without an identified bottleneck. Discrimination is established prior art, not our novelty. Proceedings abstract verified.

3. **Dwork, Feldman, Hardt, Pitassi, Reingold and Roth (NeurIPS 2015), “Generalization in Adaptive Data Analysis and Holdout Reuse.”** [Primary proceedings](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2015/hash/bad5f33780c42f2588878a9d07405083-Abstract.html). This foundational exception to the search period explains how repeatedly adapting hypotheses to holdout feedback can overfit that holdout, and provides controlled reuse mechanisms. **Implication:** after choosing a proposed revision, freeze both it and its incumbent for a separately allocated confirmation phase; later proposals must not repeatedly query the final evaluator. Keep a failed confirmation as a recorded result. **Limit:** new observations alone do not make a trajectory an IID holdout or remove policy-induced state shift. This citation motivates information-flow discipline, not a statistical guarantee for our adoption margin. Abstract and paper introduction checked; no reusable-holdout algorithm is proposed here.

4. **Ribeiro, Tiels, Umenberger, Schön and Aguirre (Automatica 2020; preprint 2019), “On the smoothness of nonlinear system identification.”** [Primary author preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.00820). The paper studies prediction-error optimization, including how noncontractive models can yield objectives whose smoothness deteriorates exponentially with simulation length; it proposes multiple shooting and compares multi-step prediction-error approaches. **Implication:** measure frozen candidate losses by rollout horizon and compare short versus longer continuous tapes at equal tool cost. Do not repeatedly reinitialize a long tape from observed intermediate states while calling it free rollout. **Limit:** this is principally an estimation/optimization result, not evidence that longer validation always selects the right model. Our ridge fit is different, and no check horizon supplies missing memory. Abstract and journal metadata verified.

5. **Janner, Fu, Zhang and Levine (NeurIPS 2019), “When to Trust Your Model: Model-Based Policy Optimization.”** [Primary author preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.08253). The method uses short model-generated rollouts branched from real data, motivated by analysis of model bias and empirical generalization. **Implication:** keep two clearly labeled diagnostics: predictions reinitialized from newly observed states, and uninterrupted predictions over an entire action sequence. Their gap can reveal accumulated rollout error that our repeated two-action restarts obscure. **Limit:** short imagined rollouts used for policy training are not a recommendation for short model-validation horizons. Its policy-return guarantees do not transfer to our investigator. Abstract verified.

6. **Kidambi, Rajeswaran, Netrapalli and Joachims (NeurIPS 2020), “MOReL: Model-Based Offline Reinforcement Learning.”** [Primary author preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.05951). MOReL constructs a pessimistic MDP to limit exploitation of an imperfect learned model, with theoretical guarantees under its assumptions. **Implication:** implement a provisional candidate state: a model can remain worth investigating without replacing the incumbent when transfer checks are unresolved. Report missed useful revisions as well as harmful adoptions. **Limit:** abstaining from adoption is our engineering analogy, not MOReL's algorithm or a safety theorem. Our committee disagreement can be small while every candidate is wrong. Abstract verified; no reward-optimization machinery recommended.

7. **Shah and colleagues (ICML 2022), “Selective Regression under Fairness Criteria.”** [Primary proceedings](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v162/shah22a.html). The authors show that lower prediction coverage need not improve every subgroup, and study conditions/regularizers addressing that disparity. **Implication:** an abstention rule must report error and acceptance counts separately for control, challenge and stress. Overall improvement achieved by refusing every difficult revision is insufficient; preserve a useful-revision measure. **Limit:** rejecting a model adoption differs from rejecting an individual prediction. The paper's subgroup guarantees are not inherited here. Proceedings abstract verified.

## Bounded experiments worth doing next

First, keep proposals and fitting fixed and compare the current check with an equal-cost split between discriminative screening and a predeclared coverage confirmation. Evaluate the same frozen candidate, not a refitted replacement. Second, if chronology is correct, vary uninterrupted tape length while preserving the action/cost budget; report horizon-specific errors and state-start differences. Third, test provisional adoption/abstention only if earlier results identify an actionable failure signal. Count retained useful challenge revisions, harmful control/stress adoptions, unresolved cases and total observation cost together. These are local design proposals, not conclusions from the papers.
